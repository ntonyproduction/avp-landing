# Marketing: Anthony's frameworks

This skill grows over time, one section per job. Every section obeys the core rules in
"Titles and headlines" section 1 (clear beats clever, steal the moment, promise only what is
delivered, select the audience, no em-dashes). When a job has no section yet, apply those
core rules and ask Anthony for his framework instead of inventing one.

# Titles and headlines

One framework, two goals. Pick the goal from the format first, then apply the shared rules
and that format's section.

| Format | What the title must do | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Shorts / vertical clips (title = the on-screen headline, same wording) | stop the scroll AND select the right viewer | 2 |
| Long-form, mids, highlights (16:9) | convert the viewer into a SUBSCRIBER, not just win the click | 3 |

Distilled from `sdsp-vibe-editing` (`resolve-finish/prompts/headline.md`, README "Headline
wording", `highlight/references/title_rules.md`) and the Cymatics headline brief. Inside
those repos, their own briefs win where they are more specific.

## 0. Before writing

Gather, per video: the transcript of the **final cut** (not the selection span, which is
often longer than what ships), who is speaking and their credential, the audience/niche, any
timeline markers or operator notes (they carry who a guest really is), and the AI/spoken hook
if the clip has one. No transcript, no title: ask for it.

## 1. Rules for every title

1. **CLEAR beats clever.** A complete statement of what the viewer gets. The reader is a
   scrolling stranger with zero knowledge of the guest, the company or the product. Test:
   reading ONLY the title, does a stranger know the **topic**, **who it's for**, and **why
   they should care**? All three, or rewrite.
   - ✗ "100 Free Lottery Tickets. Every Day." ✓ "Posting Content Is Like 100 Free Lottery Tickets"
   - ✗ "The Core Idea Behind dbt" ✓ "50 Other Tools Had This Idea First"
2. **Steal the moment.** Every title traces to a specific line in THIS transcript. Use the
   speaker's own strongest line, number or analogy. Numbers only if actually said.
3. **Promise exactly what the video delivers.** Make the clickbait true. Never claim what
   the footage contradicts (a "10-day free trial" is not "free"; a checkout page on screen
   kills a free claim). Overshooting the payoff burns trust and retention.
4. **Select the audience.** At least one word from the niche's everyday vocabulary (for
   producers: mix, beat, 808, sampling, plugin; for data/AI: model, data, AI) and the right
   **sub-domain** word, so the right people click and the algorithm files it correctly.
   - ✗ "Why Your Drop Gets Sucked Away" (reads as arrangement) ✓ "Stop Using Limiters On Your Master"
5. **Names:** an unknown house product name is never the hook; describe the capability.
   Known names (FL Studio, Whitney Houston, Tiny Desk) carry meaning and are fine. A **famous
   artist or company named in the clip goes in the title** if the clip backs it up.
   - ✗ "PLINKO Turns Your MIDI Into Falling Balls" ✓ "This Plugin Turns MIDI Into Falling Balls"
6. **Never headline a product with its function** ("Pitch Shift Any Sound With This New
   Plugin"). A spec-sheet line invites one reply, *"I can already do that"*, and the title
   ends up writing the comment section. Use an observation or a specific benefit ("No way
   they invented a plugin for this").
7. **Attribution.** No first person ("I", "my") for a guest's story: the channel presents the
   video, so "How AI Saved My Life" reads as the host's life. A guest's credential is framed
   as theirs ("Ne-Yo's Engineer Says Stop Fearing Auto-Tune").
8. **Never vague.** No blind titles ("The Best Production Tip Ever"), no vague pronouns
   ("Most Companies Don't Need This": this what?), no generic hype ("This Changed
   Everything"), no jargon the audience doesn't say casually.
9. **No em-dashes.** Use a period for a beat: two short sentences beat a dash.

## 2. Shorts headlines (title = on-screen headline)

**Deliver a slate of 3-4 candidates per clip, each a different archetype. A human picks.**
Write each clip from its own strongest moment, never one template across a batch.

| Archetype | Shape |
|---|---|
| Curiosity gap | withhold the payoff, NOT the topic ("The Mixing Mistake Killing Your Beats") |
| Bold / contrarian | invert an expectation ("Stop Mastering Your Own Tracks") |
| Number / stat | a real figure from the clip ("He Made $40K From One Loop") |
| Question | the viewer's doubt, with stakes ("Is Selling Beats Online Dead?") |
| Stakes / warning / pain | the cost of not knowing ("This Contract Clause Takes Your Royalties") |
| Mechanism / how | how it actually works ("How Placements Actually Get Picked") |
| News | timely word: new, now, finally, just changed ("Streaming Payouts Just Changed") |

The traps, each caught on a real batch:
- **Don't close the story gap.** Clear about the TOPIC, never the CONCLUSION. Specific about
  the problem, mysterious about the solution. A number that IS the answer closes the gap.
  - ✗ "90% Of Mixing Is Just Volume Levels" ✓ "Why Plugins Won't Fix Your Mix"
- **No closed yes/no questions.** The viewer answers "yes" in their head and scrolls.
  - ✗ "Did You Pick The Right Part Of Your Song?" ✓ "Why Your Best Beat Is Hiding At The End"
- **Negative trap:** a flat "shouldn't" names the rule, not its cost. Sell the pain or the
  benefit. Direct commands are fine ("Stop Adding New Sounds Every 8 Bars").
- **Steal the analogy** when the speaker hands you one ("The "Skyscraper" Rule For Making Beats").
- **Boring textbook trap:** name the benefit, not the chore.
  - ✗ "Why You Should Render Every Single Song Idea" ✓ "The 95% Rule For Finishing Your Beats"
- **Pain-framed candidates perform best** on Cymatics ("Stop doing this...", "This 1 mistake..."): include
  at least one per slate, unless the clip is a neutral explainer, where manufactured pain
  misrepresents it.
- **Headline + hook are a pair.** If the clip opens on a spoken/AI hook, the hook asks, the
  guest answers, the headline teases. The headline must not answer or repeat the hook.
- **Variety across a batch:** rotate openers and archetypes so the set doesn't read as one
  formula. A guideline, not a blocker: never weaken a headline to dodge a repeated opener;
  do differentiate adjacent clips on the same angle.

Format: **two balanced lines with an explicit `\n` break**, never three; roughly 3-5 words
per line; break on a phrase boundary; never strand a number from its unit ("Inside a 50,000 /
GPU Buildout" → "50,000 GPUs and Counting"). Write normal case; the template sets caps.

## 3. Long-form, mids, highlights (subscriber titles)

The highest-CTR styles ("Why...", vague curiosity, reaction/personality) tend to be the
**worst** subscriber converters. Chase the subscribe, not the click. Patterns, best first:

1. **"Watch this if you're [specific struggle]"** ("Watch this if you're working hard, but not making progress")
2. **"You / Your [blunt truth or command]"** ("You Need Recurring Revenue")
3. **A quoted question a viewer would also ask**, quotation marks kept ("Should I start a new business or keep my current one?", with the quotes in the title)
4. **"Helping a [niche chunked up one level] [specific tension]"** (Glamping Resort → Resort Business; name the tension: "...Charge What They're Worth", not "...Scale")
5. **"How to [specific outcome]"**
6. Number-lead: big reach, medium subscribers.

- **"Why..." is a reach play only**, used when that upload's stated goal is raw reach. (In
  Shorts, section 2, "Why" headlines are fine.)
- Defaults: a Q&A/call → the caller's quoted question (if a viewer would ask it too) or the
  "Helping a..." shape. A host teaching → "You/Your" or "How to".
- ≤ ~70 characters, one idea, fully self-contained. No formula labels ("HELPING", "WHY") in
  the final string. No "..." teasers.
- **A/B/C test slate** (when the platform allows 3 titles): A = subscriber play ("You/..." or
  quoted question), B = specific-viewer pain ("Watch this if you're..."), C = reach play (a
  contrarian claim or the single most surprising real number, never a vague tease).

## 4. Client notes

- **SDSP (Super Data Science, AI/data):** the Generalist principle. Frame around a relatable
  human outcome, not the mechanism ("NLP" → what it did for a person). Lead with the human
  element, put the guest's credential in when it earns belief, use analogies for research,
  news words for breakthroughs, name the villain for AI dangers (job loss, deepfakes, privacy).
  Two lines, ~3-5 words per line in the comic box.
- **Steven Cymatics (producers):** producer vocabulary always; never a Cymatics product name;
  never a plugin's function; famous artists in; native social framing (how-to, insider
  secret, pain) over feature lists or tagline phrasing; two balanced lines.
- **New client:** ask for the audience, the niche vocabulary and any names that must or must
  not appear before writing.

## 5. Output format

Per video:
```
Clip 03  (speaker: guest, "CDO at Candid")
  Source line: "<the transcript line the titles trace to>"
  1. [Pain]        <line 1>\n<line 2>
  2. [Mechanism]   <line 1>\n<line 2>
  3. [Number]      <line 1>\n<line 2>
  Pick: 1, because <one line>
```
Then run the checklist on the pick and say which rule any rejected candidate broke.

## 6. Checklist before a title ships

- [ ] Topic, audience and reason to care are all readable from the title alone
- [ ] Traces to a real line; every number and name is actually in the clip
- [ ] Promises only what the FINAL cut delivers; nothing the footage contradicts
- [ ] Niche + sub-domain word present; no unknown product name; no function-as-headline
- [ ] No false first person; no vague pronoun; no hype; no em-dash
- [ ] Shorts: story gap open, no closed yes/no, doesn't repeat the hook, 2 lines
- [ ] Long-form: subscriber pattern (or declared reach play), ≤ 70 chars, quotes kept

## 7. Review and learning

- **Second opinion:** the operator may run headlines through an external audit (NotebookLM
  with copywriting sources). Give it headline + hook + final-cut transcript per clip. House
  rules beat the audit: it pushes first person, YouTube-length lines, and invented specifics.
  Re-check variety after any swap.
- **Views measure the headline; retention measures the clip.** A published clip with
  above-median retention and below-median views is a **retitle**, not a re-edit. A clip
  pulled over its comments is often a retitle too: ask whether a different title would have
  prevented the pile-on.

# Copywriting

Written for email, the job it came up on. Ads, product descriptions and landing pages have no
section yet: apply the core rules and ask.

**Read this first.** Anthony sells nothing. Every tool is free, the list is small, and a welcome
sequence exists to deliver the file, teach the one feature people miss, point at the other tool,
and ask what to build next. Almost all direct-response advice assumes a paid offer and a cold
list. Section 8 lists what was left out on those grounds and why. Revisit it if a paid product
ever ships.

## 1. Voice, read off Anthony's own copy rather than imported

- **No capital letter except the first of a sentence.** Product names, apps, platforms and a
  tool's own control names all stay lowercase mid-sentence, even where the interface
  capitalises them: "Your chatterbox download", "thanks for grabbing cropduster", "in resolve",
  "windows' emoji panel", "set draw-on to 0", "ease in & out", "the choose photo button". Two
  exceptions, both in his published emails: the pronoun "I", and the one CAPITALS emphasis word
  in section 2. Image alt text follows the rule too, since a client that blocks images shows it
  in place of the picture. Subjects are sentence case. Preview text is all lowercase. Before a
  draft goes to Kit, read it for stray capitals: a control name copied off the inspector is the
  usual one.
- **First person is correct here.** "I made a second one", "I answer every one". Rule 7 bans
  first person for a *guest's* story on a client channel. It does not apply to Anthony writing
  to his own list about his own tools.
- **The product name may be the hook**, which rule 5 forbids. A welcome-sequence reader
  downloaded the tool minutes ago, so the name means something to them. Rule 5 is about
  strangers scrolling past a thumbnail.
- Sign off with the first name alone. No signature block, no company name, no titles.
- Plain text that reads as one person writing to another. No image headers, one or two links at
  most, and no styling that announces itself as a campaign.

## 2. Emphasis

- **A word in CAPITALS is the house emphasis**, and Anthony writes this way already, in this file
  as much as in his email ("Clear about the TOPIC, never the CONCLUSION").
- **Cap the word whose misreading costs the reader something**, not the word that feels important.
  That test is what stops the device inflating into shouting. The reader's eye should land exactly
  where a mistake would have cost them ten minutes: "only loads at STARTUP", "install the font
  FIRST", "disable that clip BEFORE you export", "in resolve you KEYFRAME the range".
- **Roughly one per email.** Two reads as emphasis, four reads as shouting, and it fights the quiet
  register everything else is written in: lowercase product names, no exclamation marks, a
  first-name sign-off. The caps work because the surrounding text is calm.
- **Never in a subject line.** All-caps there is a long-standing spam signal, and it is the part
  filters weigh hardest. Body copy only.
- **Bold labels, it does not shout.** Put it on the noun being handed over (the tool, the pack,
  the thing that is free) and on the single line carrying the offer, not on verbs and not on
  whatever feels strongest. Observed across a run of Cymatics emails, where bold marks the
  product every time and almost never the action. Bold plus underline plus colour reads as a
  link, so keep that combination for actual links.
- Bold is the alternative and reads as designed rather than typed. Caps read as a person emphasising
  a word out loud, which is the voice these emails are in.

## 3. Subject lines and preview text

- **Clear beats clever still governs** (rule 1), and every outside source agrees: plain direct
  language beats wordplay.
- **Trace the subject to a real line in that email**, the way a title traces to the transcript
  (rule 2).
- **Leave the story gap open**: specific about the problem, unresolved about the solution. The
  same rule as a Shorts headline, and what copywriters call a fascination.
- **Preview text is never left empty.** Kit falls back to the first line of the body and wastes
  it. Lowercase, under about 90 characters, adding to the subject instead of repeating it.
- **No em-dashes** (rule 9). No exclamation marks, no all-caps, no "FREE" in a subject line.
- A delivery email's subject is functional, not clever: "Your <tool> download", nothing more.

## 4. Body

- **The reward comes first.** The download link sits above the install instructions, because
  getting the file is why the email was opened.
- One idea per email, roughly 200 words. Short words, short sentences, short paragraphs.
- **Write about their edit, not the tool.** A feature becomes what the editor gets back: a
  caption that survives the feed, pngs never imported again.
- **Niche vocabulary is required, not simplified away.** "Adjustment clip", "inspector",
  "keyframe" and "9:16" are the audience's daily words, and they prove the tool is real.
  General readability advice says write for a fifth grader. That is for consumer lists. Keep
  sentences short, keep the nouns.
- **Claims match the build.** Version numbers, measurement dates and card counts have to be
  what actually shipped (rule 3).
- **A date needs its year, or should not be there.** Sequence emails are evergreen and get read
  months later, so "re-measured on 12 september" goes stale without anyone noticing.
- **One ask per email.** Reply, or go get the other tool. Never both in the same email.
- A P.S. is read more than the body and is worth using when there is a genuine second thing to
  say. A broadcast usually earns one. A four-line delivery email does not.
- **The ellipsis does three jobs**, and they are worth keeping apart. Trailing at the end of a
  line it withholds, so the reader has to go on to close it. Dropped mid-sentence after an
  opening word it acts as a breath, which makes the line read as spoken rather than written.
  And straddling a break it lets a sentence start in the body and finish inside the link text,
  so the link is the second half of something already being read rather than a button bolted
  on. That third one is the technique worth having; the first two are common and wear out fast.
  Observed from Cymatics rather than read off Anthony's own copy, which is the exception in
  this section: replace it the moment there is enough of his own email using it to read from.
- **What not to take from Cymatics.** Their em-dashes, FREE in a subject, capitalised product
  names and emoji in subject lines all contradict rules above, and their urgency works because
  a livestream ends. Nothing here ends: the download page is permanent. Take the structure,
  one idea per paragraph, the early link, the P.S. carrying a second thing. Leave the voice.

## 5. Storytelling and rapport

A tool email that only explains the tool is forgettable, and the sequences that carry a story are
the ones that earn a reply. But not every email wants one.

- **The test is whose problem it is.** Anthony's Capcut paragraph works because its subject is the
  reader's hesitation, not his CV: the fusion tab scares people off, so the tool goes where they
  already are. A founder's journey fails the test. The reader's own frustration, described back to
  them, passes it.
- **Where it earns its place:**
  - **Email 1**, after the download link and the install steps, never before them. Here the story
    *is* the idea, so it gets room, and section 4's 200 words bend for it.
  - **Email 2**, as one sentence explaining why that feature exists. A tip becomes a reason to
    trust the person who built it. One sentence, not a second section.
  - **Email 4**, where the origin story is load-bearing. "I got annoyed enough about it and built a
    tool" is what turns "what is the tedious part of your edit?" into a real question rather than
    market research.
- **Where it does not:** the cross-promotion email. It is the closest thing to a pitch in the
  sequence, and a story in front of the link only delays it.
- **The mission gets a different story in every tool's email 1.** The belief is the same everywhere:
  the work should happen inside the editor you are already in. The story carrying it must not be.
  Email 1 is the delivery email, so unlike email 3 it can never be filtered, which means anyone who
  downloads two tools reads both versions and a third tool makes it three. Cropduster argues the
  belief through capability, the ceiling Capcut hits. Chatterbox argues it through friction, the
  browser tab and the png import. A new tool takes whichever annoyance made it exist. One line may
  repeat deliberately as a refrain, the promise of a version for resolve and one for premiere, but
  the rest is written fresh rather than pasted.
- **A story has to be true.** Invented origin detail is worse than none: it is the one kind of
  sentence the reader might know more about than the writer, and it is unverifiable by whoever
  drafts it. Anything written on Anthony's behalf about what he did, tried or noticed gets checked
  with him before it ships. The same goes for a claim about another tool's limits, which is more
  defensible framed as his own experience than as a spec ("the first time I wanted a 3D camera move,
  it could not get me there" rather than a flat statement about what that app cannot do).

## 6. Sequences

- **Shared copy is a maintenance decision, not a writing one.** An email identical across two
  sequences reaches anyone subscribed to both twice. Filter on the other tool's tag, or accept
  the repeat with eyes open.
- **Check what else is scheduled before wording a cross-promotion.** A sequence email and a
  broadcast announcing the same tool to the same person read as a mistake.
- **Re-engaging a dormant list:** one question, no links, no images, nothing else in the email.
  "Are you still looking to [four-word desire]?"

## 7. Deliverability on a small list

- Unsubscribes are fine and the link stays easy to find. Subscribers who never open cost more
  in domain reputation than they add in list size.
- Sales and money words push mail to Promotions. Nothing is being sold, so there is no reason
  to write as though there is.
- **The real failure mode here is a funnel that delivers nothing, not a weak open rate.**
  Confirm the form, the auto-confirm setting, the tag and the sequence all exist and are
  connected before writing a word of copy for them.

## 8. Left out on purpose

From the external research, and why each was not adopted:

- **Conversion autoresponders, trials, discounts, sales calls, M-A-G-I-C offer naming.** There
  is no paid offer. Revisit if one ships.
- **"Respond to inbound leads within 60 seconds."** High-ticket sales advice. A free plugin
  download is not a sales lead.
- **A standing two-to-three emails per week cadence.** These are finite welcome sequences, not
  a newsletter. The cadence is days 0, 2, 5 and 9, then nothing.
- **"How I" in place of "How to".** Already scoped by rule 7, and section 7 warns that this
  same audit source pushes first person where it does not belong.
- **55 to 60 character line lengths.** A plain-text-era constraint. Kit reflows on mobile.
- **A third to fifth grade reading level.** Conflicts with rule 4's niche vocabulary. See
  section 4.
- **"Always A/B test subject lines."** Kit A/B tests broadcasts, not sequence emails.
- **ROI multiples and open-rate percentages.** They argue for doing the work. They do not tell
  you how to write.
